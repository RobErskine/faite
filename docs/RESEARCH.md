# Research

The cited evidence base for Faite's marketing claims. Every quote here is
**verbatim** from a primary or publisher-deposited source, with a link back to
it.

This file exists because the productivity genre runs on folklore. Half the
"facts" a landing page would reach for — 21 days to form a habit, 23 minutes to
refocus, 41% of to-dos never finished — are wrong, and the ones that are right
are usually cited to the wrong paper. Getting caught repeating one of those is
worse than saying nothing.

## How to use this file

1. **Quotes are verbatim.** Copy them exactly, or paraphrase and drop the
   quotation marks. Never paraphrase a quote and leave the citation attached to
   it.
2. **Check §4 before printing any number.** It lists the claims we deliberately
   do not make, including several that are load-bearing elsewhere in the genre.
3. **Two rows are labelled "cite as framing" or "label as an analogy."** Honour
   those labels — they mark places where a real source is being stretched past
   what it measured.
4. **§5 is not cleared for publication.** Those sources are real but were not
   confirmed to the same standard.
5. Effect sizes and caveats in the relevance column are there on purpose. A
   claim quoted without its hedge is the thing that gets challenged.

## 1. The three exits

> Faite is not a place where things go to die. It is a place where every
> captured idea eventually gets one of three honest endings: **you do it, you
> send it back, or you let it go.**

This is the framing the evidence actually supports, and it is worth stating
plainly because the obvious pitch — *"won't do" is just as good as done* — is
defensible but points at the **smaller** effect.

Barlow, Wrosch & McGrath's 2020 meta-analysis of 31 samples puts goal
**disengagement** at r = 0.08 and goal **reengagement** at r = 0.19. Letting go
helps. Redirecting helps more than twice as much. Wrosch et al. (2003) say it
directly: disengagement "is most adaptive if it leads to pursuing new
meaningful goals."

Faite already implements all three exits. Overdrive's `↓ Back to <its list>`
verdict *is* goal reengagement — the only verdict in the app that names its
destination — and the copy has never claimed it.

| Exit | Overdrive verdict | Best evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Do it** | `↑ Done` / `→ Schedule` | Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006 (d = .65 across 94 tests); Milkman et al. 2011 (date + time, +4.2pp) |
| **Send it back** | `↓ Back to <list>` | Barlow et al. 2020 (reengagement r = 0.19); Wrosch et al. 2003 |
| **Let it go** | `← Won't do` | Wrosch et al. 2003; Miller & Wrosch 2007; Brandstätter et al. 2013 |

The strongest argument for forcing the decision at all is Brandstätter's
**action crisis**: being stuck *between* persisting and dropping raised cortisol
and worsened real-world performance two weeks later. The cost is in the limbo,
not in either outcome. That is the whole case for Overflow having an exit.

## 4. Claims we do not make

Each of these is in wide circulation. Each is wrong, unsupportable, or points
the other way.

| Popular claim | What is actually true |
|---|---|
| "Your brain remembers unfinished tasks better (the Zeigarnik effect)." | A 2025 meta-analysis found **no** memory advantage for unfinished tasks: "We found no memory advantage for unfinished tasks but found a general tendency to resume tasks. … the Zeigarnik effect lacks universal validity." Frame around *intrusion and resumption*, which is what Masicampo & Baumeister actually measured. [10.1057/s41599-025-05000-w](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-025-05000-w) |
| "It takes 23 minutes 15 seconds to refocus after an interruption." | Not in Mark, González & Harris (2005), the paper it is credited to. That paper reports **25 min 26 s** as the average time before same-day resumption (sd 54 min 48 s). |
| "Task switching costs up to 40% of productive time." | The 40% figure comes from APA press material, not from Rubinstein, Meyer & Evans (2001). Do not attach it to that citation. |
| "You can only hold 7±2 things in your head." | Miller (1956) explicitly declined to unify his three "sevens," calling it "a pernicious, Pythagorean coincidence." Cite **Cowan (2001), about four chunks**. |
| "Decision fatigue — judges deny parole before lunch." | Danziger et al. (2011) is confounded. The published PNAS rebuttal shows case ordering is not random and unrepresented prisoners systematically go last. [10.1073/pnas.1110910108](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1110910108) |
| "41% of to-do list items are never completed." | Vendor telemetry from an iDoneThis ebook — no sample, no method, no peer review. The original blog URL now redirects to an unrelated acquired domain, so the primary source is not even retrievable. |
| "Writing your goals down makes you 42% more likely to achieve them." | The Gail Matthews / Dominican University study is unpublished and does not support the number as stated. Use Gollwitzer & Sheeran's d = .65 across 94 tests. |
| "Gollwitzer & Sheeran's meta-analysis covered ~8,000 participants." | Unverifiable. "94 independent tests" is verified and equally concrete. |
| "It takes 21 days to form a habit." | A myth from Maxwell Maltz's *Psycho-cybernetics* (1960), about plastic-surgery patients adjusting to their new appearance. Gardner, Lally & Wardle say so in BJGP: "Some patients may have heard that habits take 21 days to form," cited to Maltz. [10.3399/bjgp12X659466](https://doi.org/10.3399/bjgp12X659466) |
| "It takes 66 days to form a habit." | Ours to get right, since we cite the paper. It is a **median**, not an average; the range is **18–254 days**; and it describes only the 39 of 62 participants whose curve was a good model fit. Always give the range. |
| "Productivity soars below the 400 ms 'Doherty threshold'." | The 400 ms figure and the name are UX-blog accretions. The IBM report's own framing is 3 s vs 0.3 s, and the modern popularizer learned of it from the TV series *Halt and Catch Fire*. Use Liu & Heer (2014) instead. |
| "Card, Robertson & Mackinlay (1991) established the 0.1 s / 1 s / 10 s limits." | Those three sentences are **Nielsen's 1993 prose**. He cites Miller 1968 and Card et al. 1991 as the underlying research. Quote Nielsen; cite the others as background. |
| "Atkinson Hyperlegible is proven more legible." | **No study, no participant count, no effect size, no peer review** — from the Braille Institute or anyone else. The only third-party validation is a design award. Say it was *purpose-designed* for low-vision readers and is free and open source. Do not say it is proven. |
| "Nielsen's usability heuristics support undo-without-redo." | They argue the opposite: Heuristic #3's tips say "Support *Undo* and *Redo*." Faite's no-redo decision is defensible on its own terms (see `ARCHITECTURE.md` §2.11); do not borrow Nielsen for it. |
| "Science says gamification and streaks are bad." | Contested for 25 years. Cameron et al. (2001) and Cerasoli et al. (2014) push back hard on Deci et al. (1999). The defensible claim is the narrow one both camps accept: *tangible, expected rewards, loosely tied to performance, on activity people already chose.* |
| "43% of daily behaviour is habitual." | Not in Wood, Quinn & Kashy's (2002) published abstract. Do not print the percentage. |
| "Sunk cost is a robust, well-measured individual difference." | The classic Arkes & Blumer vignettes were found to have poor internal consistency (ω = 0.14–0.57) by Białek et al. (2025). The phenomenon is not overturned; the *instruments* are shaky. [10.3390/brainsci15080808](https://doi.org/10.3390/brainsci15080808) |
| "Locke & Latham's goal-setting theory supports putting it on the calendar." | Misapplication. That theory is about goal *difficulty and outcome specificity*, not *temporal* specificity. Use Milkman et al. (2011) and Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006). |
| "Letting go of goals is the big well-being win." | Overstated. Disengagement r = 0.08; **reengagement r = 0.19**. Lead with redirecting — see §1. |

**One to know about internally.** Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng (2006), the
goal-gradient paper, is real and well-evidenced — and it is the strongest
published argument *for* the progress gamification Faite deliberately omits (a
**fake** two-stamp head start on a coffee card made people finish faster).
Useful for the mechanism; be aware a critic could turn it against us.
[10.1509/jmkr.43.1.39](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.43.1.39)

## 6. How these were verified

Every quote in §2 was read from primary or publisher-deposited text, fetched
directly: Europe PMC REST and `fullTextXML`, NCBI E-utilities, Crossref,
institutional repositories, or a PDF whose text layer was decompressed locally.
Nothing came from a search-result snippet.

Publishers that block automated access — ACM DL, APA, SAGE, Taylor & Francis,
Nature, ScienceDirect, and PubMed's own HTML — were **routed around**, never
guessed at. Where no route existed, the source went to §5 instead of §2.

Two artefacts worth knowing if you re-verify from the same PDFs:

- The free Buehler et al. (1994) scan renders the italic *t* statistic as `/`,
  so the OCR reads `/(32) = 3.43`. The published text is `t(32) = 3.43`.
- The Lally et al. (2010) PDF stores word gaps as kerning rather than space
  glyphs, so raw extraction returns `was66days,witharange`. Word boundaries in
  the §2.5 quotes were restored by hand; no character was changed.

silently in a renderer rather than erroring:

- `10.1207/s15327051hci2001&2_4` (Bellotti 2005) contains an **ampersand**,

- `10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1` (Gollwitzer & Sheeran) contains

  This one carried the `d = .65` figure, the most-quoted statistic in the file.

*text* still shows the human-readable DOI. **If you edit either row, keep the
encoding.**
